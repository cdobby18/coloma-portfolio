Chapter I — Still Figuring It Out

Carl Joshua Coloma · Journey · 2026
Writing

I'm a computer science student specializing in software engineering, and if I'm being honest, "aspiring AI engineer" still feels like a big title for someone who spends half his time confused. But that's the goal I'm chasing, so here's the actual story, not the polished version.

I fell into tutorial hell early. Course after course, video after video, copying along and feeling productive without really building anything on my own. It took a while to admit that finishing a tutorial isn't the same as understanding it.

Watching someone else build something is not the same as building it yourself.

There's also this constant feeling of not progressing the right way. Everyone online seems three steps ahead, shipping projects, landing roles, explaining concepts I'm still shaky on. Some days I genuinely feel clueless, like I'm just rearranging the same beginner knowledge instead of moving forward.

The thing that actually helps is staying curious and not being afraid to ask. During my internship I learned to just keep asking my mentors questions, even the basic ones, even when I could tell it got a little irritating. It's fine. As long as I'm learning something from it, the mild annoyance is worth it.

I don't have a clean ending for this yet. I'm not an AI engineer. I'm a student trying to become one, one confusing concept at a time. If you're in the same spot, tutorial hell and all, I figured it might help to know you're not the only one.

Chapter II — BAWZ: Dance Group

In August 2025 I competed with my dance group BAWZ at an international event in Singapore. We represented the Philippines against teams from across Asia, and we came home with the champion award.

BAWZ is not just a group; it's a team built around discipline, rhythm, and trust. Our coach pushed us through long rehearsals, and every routine had to be precise enough to stand out on an international stage.

The moment we heard that we had won was unforgettable. We had trained for months, and the award validated every injury, every early practice, and every late-night rehearsal.

We took the stage against teams from different countries, and our performance brought home the trophy. It showed what a Philippine crew can do when we combine creativity, precision, and cultural pride.

That win is part of my story too. It taught me how teamwork and preparation pay off, and it gave me confidence that I can represent the Philippines at a high level — whether on a stage, in a project, or in a technical role.

Chapter III — The First Build

The first build is the smallest part of my story — and somehow the most important one. No grand adventure, no glory, no legend. Just me, a fresh graduate with a computer science degree and a head full of theory, deciding enough was enough.

My first build was the first thing I ever built on my own. I decided to set sail on my first AI workflow — and I didn't wait until I felt ready. I just built the thing I wanted to build, badly, gloriously badly.

The first workflow was a mess. It worked. It didn't work. It worked again. And in that messy loop was the same lesson I found in every tutorial I ever watched: building is understanding, and understanding comes from doing.

You can watch a thousand tutorials, but you only learn by building.

For anyone my age or older, staring at a finished degree and an unfinished skills list: I get it. The leap from "I know about AI" to "I can build with AI" felt enormous. It did for me too. But the first build doesn't ask for a grand plan. It asks for one small thing. Mine was a workflow. Yours might be an agent, a RAG pipeline, a fine-tune, or just a very stubborn notebook.

I didn't become an AI engineer with that first build. I just became someone who builds — and that still inspires me to keep going today.

Chapter IV — The Voyage Begins

The AI engineering journey started with a simple thing: watching. At first, I was just absorbing — hours of professional software engineering and AI content online, the deep technical breakdowns from creators like Gazi, Bryl, and Daniel, full of pipelines, agents, and production-grade thinking.

Most of it went in one ear and out the other at first. But something about their code, their breakdowns, and the "why" behind the "how" planted a seed. If they could build these systems, I could start building mine.

At first I was watching. Then I decided to build.

That inspiration pushed me from passive learning to action. I stopped admiring the voyage and started sketching my own first workflow — badly, gloriously badly, exactly like Chapter III. The journey from "watching" to "doing" became the real story.

And that's the heart of it: the AI engineering journey began with a spark of inspiration from creators I watched online, and it became my own because I chose to do my best.
